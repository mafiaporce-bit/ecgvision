iStandard STEMI activation

New ST elevation at the J-point in 2 or more contiguous leads (V3R and V4R included):

  • V2–V3: men under 40 ≥2.5 mm, men 40 and over ≥2 mm, women of any age ≥1.5 mm
  • All other leads: ≥1 mm
  • Only valid without LV hypertrophy or LBBB

Signifies ongoing acute coronary artery occlusion.

Contiguous lead groups

  • Inferior: II, III, aVF
  • Lateral: I, aVL, V5, V6
  • Septal: V1, V2
  • Anterior: V3, V4
  • Posterior: V7–V9 · Right ventricle: V3R, V4R

iiPosterior STEMI

ST depression in V1–V3, especially when the terminal T wave is positive (STE equivalent), plus ST elevation ≥0.5 mm in V7–V9.

iiiLCx occlusion / right ventricular MI

ST elevation in V7–V9 (left circumflex), or in V3R and V4R (right ventricle).

ivMultivessel ischaemia / left main obstruction

ST depression ≥1 mm in 6 or more surface leads (inferolateral), with ST elevation in aVR and/or V1. Especially significant with haemodynamic compromise.

vLBBB / paced rhythm

QRS over 120 ms. No Q waves in I, V5, V6. Monomorphic R in I, V5, V6. ST and T opposite the main QRS deflection. With high clinical suspicion of ischaemia, manage as STEMI.

viRBBB

QRS over 120 ms. rsR' "bunny ear" in V1–V3. Slurred S waves in I, aVL and often V5–V6. With high clinical suspicion of ischaemia, manage as STEMI.

Service rule: if any of these patterns is seen, the app shows a yellow alert. Call UMOC 02 504 8555 to discuss STEMI PPCI activation.

WiLLiaM: left bundle branch block

  • V1: "W" shape, a deep wide QS or rS.
  • V6: "M" shape, a broad notched R with no Q wave.
  • QRS over 120 ms, ST and T opposite the main QRS deflection.
  • Standard STE criteria don't apply. Use modified Sgarbossa (Smith).

MaRRoW: right bundle branch block

  • V1: "M" shape, rsR' bunny ears.
  • V6: "W" shape, a wide slurred S wave.
  • QRS over 120 ms. STE criteria still apply in RBBB.

Quick tell from V1

Look at the terminal part of the QRS in V1. Deflection pointing down means LBBB; pointing up means RBBB.

Learn ECG the easy way

15 small steps. Read one step, look at the picture, tap Got it. That's it. No step is hard on its own.

0 of 15 done

Your heart is a pump. Before each squeeze, a tiny spark of electricity runs through it, like a wave going through a stadium crowd.

The ECG machine "listens" to that electricity through stickers on the skin and draws it as a line.

Think of it like: a doorbell. The spark rings first, then the heart squeezes. No spark, no squeeze.
  • Line goes up = electricity coming toward that sticker.
  • Line goes down = electricity going away from it.
  • Line flat = nothing happening right now.

A normal heart, beat after beat. Every bump is part of one heartbeat.

  • P wave = the little hill. The top rooms (atria) squeeze.
  • QRS = the big tall spike. The big bottom rooms (ventricles) squeeze. This is the real pump.
  • T wave = the gentle hill. The heart rests and recharges for the next beat.
Remember: P = Prepare, QRS = Squeeze, T = Take a rest.
  • 1 small box = 1 mm = 0.04 seconds.
  • 1 big box = 5 small boxes = 0.20 seconds.
  • 5 big boxes = 1 second.
  • Going up: 10 small boxes (10 mm) = 1 mV. We measure ST in mm (small boxes up or down).
Think of it like: a ruler. Going sideways measures time. Going up and down measures strength.

This is true when the paper runs at the normal speed: 25 mm/s and 10 mm/mV (printed at the bottom of the ECG).

Find a tall spike (R wave) sitting on a thick line. Then count the thick lines until the next spike, saying:

300150100756050

Where the next spike lands is the heart rate.

The next spike lands on 75, so the rate is about 75 per minute.

Beat not regular? Use the 6-second trick: count the spikes in 30 big boxes (6 seconds) and multiply by 10. On a full 12-lead printout (10 seconds), count all the spikes in the long strip at the bottom and multiply by 6.
  • Under 60 = slow (bradycardia).
  • 60 to 100 = normal.
  • Over 100 = fast (tachycardia).

Regular means the spikes are the same distance apart, like a clock: tick, tick, tick.

  1. Put a piece of paper under the long strip.
  2. Mark the tops of 2 or 3 spikes on it.
  3. Slide the paper along. If the marks still land on spikes, it's regular.

Regular: every gap is the same.

Irregular: the gaps are all different, with no pattern. This one is atrial fibrillation (step 12).

The heart's own spark plug is called the sinus node. If it's in charge, the answer to all 4 is YES:

  1. Is there a P wave (little hill) before every QRS?
  2. Is there a QRS after every P wave?
  3. Is the P wave pointing up in lead II?
  4. Is it regular?

All yes + rate 60–100 = normal sinus rhythm. Slow = sinus bradycardia. Fast = sinus tachycardia.

Sinus bradycardia: normal beats, just far apart (about 50).

Sinus tachycardia: normal beats, close together (about 125).

  • PR (start of P to start of QRS) = 3 to 5 small boxes (0.12–0.20 s). Longer = the message is slow getting through (a heart block, step 13).
  • QRS (the spike's width) = less than 3 small boxes (under 0.12 s) = narrow, normal.
  • QRS 3 or more small boxes = wide. Think: bundle branch block, a beat starting in the ventricle, a pacemaker, or high potassium.
  • QT (start of QRS to end of T): easy rule, it should be less than half the gap between two spikes.
Think of it like: a relay race. PR is the baton pass, QRS is the sprint. A slow pass = long PR. A slow, wobbly sprint = wide QRS.

A 12-lead ECG takes 12 pictures of the same heartbeat from 12 different sides. Leads that look at the same wall are neighbors (same color).

IaVRV1V4 IIaVLV2V5 IIIaVFV3V6

This is how the leads sit on most printouts.

  • Inferior (bottom wall): II, III, aVF
  • Lateral (left side wall): I, aVL, V5, V6
  • Septal (middle wall): V1, V2
  • Anterior (front wall): V3, V4
  • aVR: looks from the right shoulder. Usually everything points down here.
Extra cameras when needed: V7–V9 look at the back (posterior). V3R, V4R look at the right ventricle.

Look only at lead I and lead aVF. Is the QRS mostly up (thumb up 👍) or mostly down (thumb down 👎)?

Shortcut: most monitors print the axis number at the top. Normal is about −30° to +90°.

The ST segment is the short line right after the big spike, before the T wave. Normally it sits flat on the baseline (the flat line before the P wave).

  • ST elevation (lifted up): a heart artery may be blocked right now. That's a STEMI.
  • ST depression (pushed down): the heart is short of oxygen, OR it's a mirror of elevation somewhere else (step 11).

How to measure: find the J point (where the spike ends and the ST begins). Count the small boxes between it and the baseline. 1 small box = 1 mm.

Think of it like: a water pipe. A blocked heart pipe makes the ST line "lift up" in the cameras looking at that wall.

How much is enough? (your service rule, ESC 2023)

  • 1 mm or more in 2 neighbor leads (any leads except V2–V3).
  • In V2–V3: men 40+ = 2 mm, men under 40 = 2.5 mm, women = 1.5 mm.

Rule 1: Neighbors. One lead alone doesn't count. The ST must be up in 2 or more neighbor leads (same color in step 8).

Rule 2: Mirrors. When one wall shows ST up, the camera on the opposite side often shows ST down. That's called reciprocal change. It makes a real STEMI more likely.

I ↓aVRV1V4 II ↑aVL ↓V2V5 III ↑aVF ↑V3V6

Example: ST up in II, III, aVF (red) + mirror ST down in I and aVL (blue) = inferior STEMI.

  • Inferior ST up → look for a mirror in aVL / I.
  • Anterior ST up (V1–V4) → look for a mirror in II, III, aVF.
  • ST down in V1–V3 with tall R waves → think posterior: do V7–V9.
  • Inferior STEMI → do V4R to check the right ventricle.

Sinus rhythm

P before every QRS, regular, 60–100.

Atrial fibrillation (AF)

No clear P waves, wobbly baseline, spikes at random distances.

Atrial flutter

Saw-tooth waves between the spikes. Often a rate of about 150.

SVT

Fast, narrow, very regular (often over 150). P waves hidden.

Ventricular tachycardia (VT)

Fast and wide, big repeating waves. Check for a pulse! No pulse = shockable.

Ventricular fibrillation (VF)

Total chaos, no real beats. Patient has no pulse. Shockable.

Asystole

Almost flat line. Check the leads are on, increase the size (gain) and check another lead. Not shockable.

Paced rhythm

A thin straight pacemaker spike before each wide QRS.

Sinus brady / tachy

Normal-looking beats, just slow (under 60) or fast (over 100). See step 6.

Treat every rhythm per your Clinical Guidelines. The patient comes first: always check the pulse and the patient, not just the screen.

Think of it like: a messenger (P) carrying a letter to the boss (QRS). The PR gap is the walking time.

1st degree

The messenger is slow but always arrives. PR longer than 5 small boxes, every P has a QRS.

2nd degree type 1 (Wenckebach)

The messenger gets more and more tired: PR gets longer, longer, longer… then one P has no QRS. Then it starts again.

2nd degree type 2

PR stays the same, but suddenly a P has no QRS. More dangerous.

3rd degree (complete block)

The messenger and the boss don't talk at all. P waves march on their own, QRS march on their own (slow, often wide). Dangerous: pads on, call UMOC.

Do it in the same order every time. Same as the app does.

  1. Patient first. Pulse? Pain? Sick or not sick?
  2. Rate: 300 trick or 6-second trick. Slow, normal or fast?
  3. Regular? Paper trick.
  4. P before every QRS? Sinus or not?
  5. Gaps: PR 3–5 small boxes? QRS narrow (under 3)?
  6. Axis: thumbs in I and aVF.
  7. ST: up or down? Measure at the J point in mm.
  8. Neighbors + mirrors: 2 leads on the same wall? Mirror on the opposite side?
  9. Tricky ones: LBBB, paced, LVH, or a wide QRS can hide or fake a STEMI. Check the OMI patterns too. Not sure? Call UMOC.
